Question 1

What Google Ads is accountable for

Google Ads is a demand capture channel. It does not create the intent — it competes for intent that already exists and converts it as efficiently as possible. Everything inside that boundary is ours. Everything outside it is not, and a CSA who blurs the line will end up defending a number they don't control.

What we can be held to
  • Query quality — are we paying for searches a buyer would make?
  • The click — CTR, ad copy, extensions, ad strength, position
  • Query-to-page match — does the page answer the search?
  • Conversion rate on the traffic we send
  • Cost per conversion and the share of spend that was wasted
  • Clean measurement — the right conversion actions, firing correctly
What we do not control
  • How many people search — search volume is a ceiling, not a lever
  • Who is behind the keyboard — we buy the query, not the person
  • The offer and whether the market wants it
  • Sales follow-up, speed to lead, and close rate
  • The rest of the website beyond the page we point at

The nuance that matters: we can only be accountable for cost per qualified lead when the CRM sends qualification back to Google. Without that loop we are accountable for cost per conversion — and a CSA should say which one out loud before quoting a number.

On the limits of search targeting
Question 2

What Google is supposed to do — and not do — next to the other channels

Google captures demand. LinkedIn, content and outbound create it. Run Google alone and you are betting that enough strangers will type the right words this month. Run both and the same money works twice: LinkedIn primes the buyer, Google catches them at the moment of intent — and then Google takes all the credit.

Google is the right channel for
  • Buyers already searching the category
  • Branded search — and defending it from competitors
  • Short-cycle, direct-response capture
  • Reading what the market actually calls the problem
Google is the wrong channel for
  • Brand awareness at scale
  • Educating a market that isn't searching yet
  • Reaching a named account list or job title
  • Being the only channel and still hitting a pipeline number
The CSA translation

If a client wants to cut LinkedIn and put it all into Google, the honest answer is that non-brand volume is capped by search demand and brand volume is capped by how many people have heard of them. Cutting demand gen shrinks the ceiling of the channel they're doubling down on.

"Google usually suffers if LinkedIn is not done, because you don't have that other part of the equation. You are relying on someone sitting behind a keyword that they will type the right stuff. So it is not the whole equation."

The one that trips people up

A low average CPC is not a win. When we tighten targeting to phrase and exact on the terms that actually convert, CPC goes up and impressions go down — while conversions go up. Cheap clicks usually mean we are winning auctions nobody valuable is in. What I want to see over a quarter: impressions down, clicks down, conversions up.

  • Ad strength — Good or Excellent, never Poor. Anything at grade 7+ is fine. Excellent buys cheaper clicks and more impressions.
  • Brand vs non-brand split — always report them separately. Blending them hides a broken non-brand campaign behind a healthy brand campaign.
A blended number that sits inside the benchmark is not proof of health. Split brand out first, then judge.

How to frame stage 2 to a client
Question 4 · Mistake 1 of 5
Most common · most expensive

Broad match with no guardrails

Broad match is where B2B budgets go to die. Google will happily spend a $6,000 budget on people looking for jobs, free templates and training courses, because those queries are "topically related" to what the client sells. On a $3–10K budget you cannot afford topical.

Our policy

Phrase and exact first. Broad match is a test — narrow scope, capped budget, clear success criteria — never the default. If broad match is carrying a meaningful share of spend on an account, that is a finding, not a footnote.

Why phrase and exact wins on a tight budget
  • The budget stays on queries a buyer would actually type
  • Copy and landing page can be written to one theme, which lifts ad strength and lowers CPC
  • Spend concentrates enough to teach the algorithm something real
  • Reporting stays clean — you can tell the client which themes work
Volume discipline that goes with it

On a small budget I cap keyword selection around 30 high-intent terms and avoid anything much above 200–300 searches/month. A 4,400/month head term looks attractive on the plan and then eats the budget without ever holding position. We will not have the money to stay in front of all of that traffic.

Match type, in practice
Default Exact

Proven, high-value terms. Highest control. Monthly SQR is enough.

Default Phrase

The workhorse for discovering new intent. Watch for token drop — Google will quietly discard "software" or "platform". Bi-weekly SQR.

Test only Broad

Capped budget, tight audience filters, weekly SQR, and a date it gets judged. Never the account's main engine.

Question 4 · Mistake 2 of 5
The one that makes every other diagnosis unreliable

Never letting the account have a steady week

This is the first thing I check and the mistake I see most often in accounts that "aren't working." Someone is anxious, so every few days something changes — budget, bid strategy, a new ad group, a paused keyword. The account is permanently in learning. There is no clean period to read, so every conclusion drawn from it is guesswork.

W1
Week 1
  • Budget raised 40% after a slow start
  • Two new ad groups added
W2
Week 2
  • CPA looks high → switched to Max Conversions
  • Learning phase restarts
W3
Week 3
  • CPC spikes during learning → budget cut back
  • Three keywords paused mid-learning
W4
Week 4
  • Client asks why performance is inconsistent
  • No four-week window exists to answer with
Four weeks of spend, zero weeks of signal. The account did not underperform — it was never allowed to perform.
What I do instead

Pick one: freeze the account for 15–20 days and read a clean period, or do the whole revamp now — structure, budgets, bidding, conversions, all at once — then let it learn undisturbed for 15 days and judge it after. What you cannot do is drip changes in and expect a readable result.

The question I ask myself before any change: how much more learning do we want to buy? Do we want something that brings leads, or something that brings more learning?

The CSA move
  • Open the change history before the performance tab. If there is no unchanged 7-day window, that is your first finding and it outranks every metric on the screen.
  • Say it to the client as a decision, not an excuse. "We're holding the account steady through the 18th so the next report is measured on clean data" is a plan. "It's still learning" is a hedge.
  • One variable at a time once you're past the revamp. Otherwise you will never know which change worked.

"For the last four weeks I believe we didn't have one steady week without any changes. So either we do not touch anything for the next 15 to 20 days, or we do the whole revamp now and let it learn for 15 days."

Question 4 · Mistakes 3–5

Three more that show up in almost every audit

Mistake 3

Optimizing to the wrong conversion

Newsletter signups, pricing-page views and PDF downloads all marked Primary. Smart bidding does exactly what you asked: it finds more of the cheapest action on the list. Now 95% of "conversions" are worthless and the bidding algorithm has been trained to chase them.

What to check
  • How many Primary conversion actions exist?
  • Does each one signal intent to buy?
  • Is one action producing 90% of the volume?
Highest-leverage fix in any audit
Mistake 4

Budget spread too thin to learn

Six campaigns and twenty ad groups on $4K. Nothing accumulates enough conversions to optimize, so everything sits in permanent learning and every segment looks mediocre. On a small budget: one non-brand campaign with several themed ad groups, find the winner, then promote it into its own campaign.

The bonus this structure gives you

When one ad group clearly outperforms and you can show it, you have a real argument for a budget increase — "this theme works, here is what more of it costs" — instead of asking for money on faith.

Also: search partners off, auto-apply off
Mistake 5

Sending the query somewhere it isn't answered

The ad group is about failover connectivity; the page is about the partner program. The click is fine, the copy is fine, and the visitor bounces because the page does not answer the search they just made. This shows up as a conversion-rate problem and gets misdiagnosed as an ads problem.

What good looks like
  • A dedicated page per theme, not the homepage
  • No nav — the only clickable things are the CTA and the logo
  • H1 echoes the query language
  • No custom-domain redirects between ad and page
Query → page match also drives Quality Score
Mistakes 3 and 5 both surface as "our conversion rate is bad." They have completely different fixes. Which is why the next slide exists.

Question 6

What only Google can tell you

Every channel produces performance data. Google produces something no other channel does: the exact words a buyer used to describe their own problem, unprompted, at the moment they were looking to solve it. That is the most valuable output of the channel and it is routinely thrown away.

Insight 1 · Voice of market, for free

The search terms report is a live transcript of demand. Keyword tools tell you what people search; the SQR tells you what your buyers search. Conversions concentrating on one phrasing is a positioning finding, not just a bidding one.

A real example: one client's conversions clustered hard on "wholesale" variants while the adjacent phrasing produced clicks and nothing else. The takeaway wasn't "raise the bid" — it was the market likes the word wholesale. That belongs in their LinkedIn copy, their homepage H1, and their sales deck.

Insight 2 · Where the demand actually is
  • Impression share by theme shows unmet demand — a converting theme at 30% IS means we are visible for less than a third of the buyers looking for it
  • Lost to budget vs lost to rank separates "we need more money" from "we need better ads" — two very different client conversations
  • Auction insights and the Ads Transparency Center show who else is bidding and roughly how heavily they're investing
Insight 3 · Non-converting traffic is not always waste

A theme with a 10% CTR and no conversions is not automatically a pause. Those are qualified people raising their hand — they land on the site, enter the retargeting pool, and get converted later by LinkedIn. Judged on Google's own conversion column it looks like a failure; judged across the funnel it may be feeding the channel that closes.

The discipline: keep it deliberately and say why, or pause it deliberately and say why. What you cannot do is keep it by accident.

Insight 4 · The intent ceiling is a real number

Google is the only channel that will tell a client the size of their in-market demand. If the whole high-intent keyword set is 900 searches a month in their geography, no amount of budget produces 50 demos from search. That is a genuinely useful, genuinely uncomfortable strategic input — and it is the strongest argument for demand gen that exists.

"We come in, we get 500 identified visitors, and then they do literally nothing with them. I ask if they have outreach sequences, if we want to retarget them, if we want to do anything for this group — and they have no idea."

The most common wasted asset in an account
If a CSA leaves a monthly report having only discussed cost per lead, they gave away the most valuable thing the channel produced that month.

Question 7 · continued

The three dead ends I walked into on the way

The brief asked for the dead ends, and they are the most useful part of a teardown — because a CSA will hit exactly these three, in this order, and each one costs real money to get wrong.

Say these out loud in the training — the reasoning is the lesson, not the answer
Dead end 1

"CVR is 0.93%, so it's the landing page." It's the first place everyone goes. But brand traffic converts at 3.41% on that same page. The page converts fine — for qualified traffic. If I had rebuilt the page I'd have spent three weeks and a design budget fixing something that wasn't broken.

Dead end 2

"Mid-intent CTR is 1.90%, so the copy is weak." Checked it: ad strength Good, 12 headlines, keyword insertion on, two RSAs per group. The copy is fine. Nobody clicks because the query has nothing to do with the product. You cannot write your way out of the wrong keyword.

Dead end 3

"Competitor spent $590 for zero — pause it." Tempting, and wrong. 19% impression share on 75 clicks is not a fair trial. Compare with the mid-intent campaign: $2,760 for one demo. That has enough evidence. Pause on evidence, not on impatience.

Question 8 · The handoff

One question a CSA must always be able to answer

"Do I know why the number is what it is — and can I name the one thing we're changing to move it?"

Clients do not need you to be a Google Ads specialist. They need you to be the person who knows what happened and why, and who does not get surprised. If you can answer that question, you can hold any Google conversation. If you can't, you are reading a dashboard out loud and they can feel it.

Three sub-checks before any call
  • Is the data clean? Was there a steady week, and are the conversion actions the right ones? If not, that is your headline — not the CPL.
  • Which link broke? Query, click, page, form, or CRM. Name one. CTR and conversion rate will tell you which.
  • What are we changing, and what should they expect to see first? Usually cost per lead moves before volume. Say that before they ask.
The credibility habits underneath it
  • Never a metric without a range and a verdict. "4.65% — the benchmark for your industry is 3 to 5% — so we're inside it." Value, range, meaning. Every time.
  • Flag the bad news before they find it. Underspend, a paused campaign, a bad week — you raise it, framed as a decision. It buys you the benefit of the doubt on everything else.
  • Ask for lead quality every single call. "Did any of these move through the funnel?" It is the only way to know if we're actually doing a good job, and it makes them a participant rather than an auditor.
  • Don't overpromise the number. Commit to what you're confident in and beat it.
  • Turn objections into segmentation questions. "$500 a lead is too expensive" isn't a number to defend — it's a prompt to ask which segment is worth $500 and go get more of that one.
  • Earn the budget increase with evidence. Conversion volume plus confirmed lead quality plus impression-share headroom. Not "we'd like more."

"It is more of installing trust — they need to trust what you are saying."

Presenting the monthly Google report

Same account, same numbers — now the job is a thirty-minute client call that ends with them trusting the plan.

Part 2 · Structure

How I walk a client through a Google month

Nine beats, in this order. The order matters more than the slides: it puts the channel's job before its numbers, the numbers before the verdict, and the verdict before the ask.

01
Set the frame and invite interruption

"I won't go point by point — the report's yours after the call. Stop me anywhere." Then state what the channel is for: it's here to capture demand from people already searching for what you do. Everything after is judged against that job.

02
Headline numbers, each with a range and a verdict

Spend, leads, cost per lead, CTR, conversion rate. Never a bare number: value, industry range, "so that means." Five numbers is enough — resist the dashboard dump.

03
Flag the bad news yourself, framed as a decision

Underspend, a paused campaign, a soft week. "We spent $4,500 of the $5,000 on purpose — the last $500 would have raised cost per lead." Raising it first is the single biggest trust-builder on the call. Finding it themselves is the single biggest destroyer.

04
Split brand and non-brand, always

And pre-empt the obvious question: brand looks better because brand is limited — far fewer people search your name than search the category, and non-brand is simply harder. That framing stops "why don't we just do more brand?"

05
Where the money went — and what got paused, with the reasoning

"This ad group spent $763 over 90 days with no conversions, so we paused it to free room for the ones that are converting." Every pause needs a justification and every increase needs evidence. This is also where you show the winner you want to fund.

Part 2 · Structure, continued

Beats six through nine — where the call is actually won

The first five beats report the month. These four decide whether they trust you with next month's budget.

06
What we learned about their market

The search-term insight. Which words converted, which phrasing the market prefers, which competitor is bidding harder. This is the part of the report they cannot get anywhere else, and it's the part that makes you a strategist instead of a vendor.

07
Ask about lead quality — every call, without exception

"How did these leads look? Did any move through the funnel?" It's the only real measure of whether we're doing a good job, and it turns the client into a collaborator. It also gives you the data you need to defend cost per lead next month.

08
What we're changing next, and what to expect first

Two or three actions, with a date. Set the expectation about sequence: after a structural change, cost per lead usually moves before volume, and there'll be a learning period. Say it now so it isn't a surprise in four weeks.

09
The budget ask — gated on evidence, or not made at all

Only if you have all three: conversion volume, confirmed lead quality, and impression-share headroom on something that's converting. "This theme converts at $180 and we're only visible for 38% of those searches — here's what closing that gap costs." If you don't have all three, don't ask.

Part 2 · The hard moments

Four things a client will say, and what to do with them

These four cover most of what actually happens on a Google call. Notice the pattern in all four responses: don't defend the number. Reframe to segment, timeline, or the boundary of what the channel controls.

"The phone isn't ringing."

Don't argue with the feeling — they're telling you something real about their experience even if the data disagrees. Acknowledge it, then move to timeline and mechanics: what the account has been doing, what has already improved, what's changing this month. Then close the loop by asking what they are seeing come through, so you can find the gap between their inbox and your dashboard. Nine times out of ten it's attribution or a form routing problem.

"$500 a lead isn't worth it for us."

Concede immediately if their unit economics say so — arguing with a client's own maths destroys credibility. Then convert it into a segmentation question: which segment is worth $500, and which one is worth $150? That's the conversation that produces a better account. "That's exactly why we're concentrating on the wholesale ad groups" is a much stronger answer than a defence of the blended figure.

"These leads were bad."

Take it seriously, get specifics, and be honest about the structural limit: on search we buy the query, not the person — we cannot choose who is behind the keyboard. Then show the levers we do have: negative keywords, tighter match types, audience exclusions, and a form gate on business email. Give them a concrete change and a date. Never promise 100% quality; promise a tighter filter.

"Should we just cut this and put it all into the other channel?"

Answer with the ceiling, not with loyalty. Non-brand volume is capped by how many people search the category; brand volume is capped by how many people have heard of them. If they cut demand gen, they shrink the ceiling of the channel they're doubling down on — Google gets the credit for conversions that demand gen primed. Show the search-volume number. It's the most honest argument you have and it doesn't sound like a pitch.

And one you should say yourself, before they do

"Here's what I wasn't happy with this month." Naming your own miss — a slow deliverable, a change that didn't work, a week you lost — costs you very little and buys you enormous latitude on strategy. Clients forgive process problems. They do not forgive being managed.
