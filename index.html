Question 1

What Google Ads is accountable for

Google Ads captures demand that already exists. It does not create it. Everything inside that boundary is ours — everything outside it is not.

We are accountable for
  • The queries we pay for — would a buyer search this?
  • The click — CTR, ad copy, ad strength
  • Query-to-page match — does the page answer the search?
  • Conversion rate on the traffic we send
  • Cost per lead and how much spend was wasted
  • Clean tracking — the right conversions, firing properly
We are not accountable for
  • How many people search — that is a ceiling, not a lever
  • Who is behind the keyboard — we buy the query, not the person
  • The offer, and whether the market wants it
  • Sales follow-up and close rate

Question 2

Where Google sits next to the other channels

Google captures demand. LinkedIn and content create it. Run Google alone and you are betting enough strangers type the right words this month. Run both and the same money works twice — then Google takes the credit.

Google is right for
  • Buyers already searching the category
  • Branded search, and defending it
  • Short-cycle, direct response
Google is wrong for
  • Brand awareness at scale
  • Educating a market that isn't searching
  • Reaching a named account list
When a client wants to cut LinkedIn

Non-brand volume is capped by search demand. Brand volume is capped by how many people have heard of them. Cutting demand gen shrinks the ceiling of the channel they want to double down on.

Question 4 · Mistakes 1 and 2

The two that cost the most

Mistake 1

Broad match with no guardrails

Google will spend a $6,000 budget on people looking for jobs, free templates and training courses, because those queries are loosely related to what the client sells. On a B2B budget you cannot afford loosely related.

Our policy

Phrase and exact first. Broad match is a test — small scope, capped budget, a date it gets judged. If broad is carrying real spend in an account, that is a finding.

Why it wins on a tight budget
  • Spend stays on queries a buyer would actually type
  • Copy and page can be written to one theme — better ad strength, lower CPC
  • Spend concentrates enough for the algorithm to learn something real
Keyword volume discipline

On a small budget: around 30 high-intent keywords, avoiding anything much above 200–300 searches a month. A 4,400/month head term looks good on the plan and then eats the budget without ever holding position.

Mistake 2

Never letting the account have a steady week

Someone is anxious, so every few days something changes — budget, bid strategy, a new ad group, a paused keyword. The account is permanently in learning, so there is no clean period to read and every conclusion is a guess.

Four weeks of spend, zero weeks of signal. The account did not underperform — it was never allowed to perform.
The fix is a choice, not a compromise

Either freeze the account for 15–20 days and read a clean period, or do the whole rebuild now — structure, budgets, bidding, conversions together — then leave it alone for 15 days. What does not work is dripping changes in and expecting a readable result.

Open the change history before the performance tab. If there is no unchanged 7-day window, that is the first finding and it outranks every metric on the screen.
Question 4 · Mistakes 3 to 5

Three more that show up in almost every audit

Mistake 3

Optimizing to the wrong conversion

Newsletter signups, pricing page views and PDF downloads all set as primary. Smart bidding does what it was told and finds more of the cheapest action on the list. Now most "conversions" are worthless and the algorithm has been trained to chase them.

Check
  • How many primary conversion actions exist?
  • Does each one mean intent to buy?
  • Is one action producing 90% of the volume?
Highest-leverage fix in any audit
Mistake 4

Budget spread too thin to learn

Six campaigns and twenty ad groups on $4K. Nothing gathers enough conversions to optimize, so everything sits in learning and every segment looks mediocre.

Instead

One non-brand campaign with a few themed ad groups. Find the winner, then promote it into its own campaign. That also gives you a real argument for more budget — "this theme works, here is what more of it costs."

Search partners off · auto-apply off
Mistake 5

Sending the query somewhere it isn't answered

The ad group is about one product; the page is about another. The click is fine, the copy is fine, and the visitor leaves because the page does not answer what they just searched. It looks like a conversion rate problem and gets blamed on the ads.

Good looks like
  • A dedicated page per theme, not the homepage
  • No navigation — only the CTA and the logo are clickable
  • The headline echoes the search
Also drives Quality Score
Mistakes 3 and 5 both show up as "our conversion rate is bad." They have completely different fixes.

Question 6

What only Google can tell you

Every channel produces performance data. Google produces something no other channel does — the exact words a buyer used to describe their own problem, at the moment they were looking to solve it.

The words the market actually uses

Keyword tools tell you what people search. The search terms report tells you what your buyers search. When conversions cluster on one phrasing, that is a positioning finding, not a bidding one — it belongs in their LinkedIn copy, their homepage headline and their sales deck.

On one account, conversions clustered hard on one word while the adjacent phrasing produced clicks and nothing else. The takeaway was not "raise the bid." It was that the market prefers that word.

Where the demand actually is
  • Impression share by theme shows unmet demand — a converting theme at 30% means we are visible to less than a third of the buyers looking for it
  • Lost to budget vs lost to rank separates "we need more money" from "we need better ads" — two very different client conversations
  • The Ads Transparency Center shows who else is bidding and roughly how heavily
Traffic that doesn't convert isn't always waste

A theme with a 10% CTR and no conversions is not automatically a pause. Those are qualified people landing on the site and entering the retargeting pool, where LinkedIn converts them later. Keep it on purpose, or pause it on purpose — but not by accident.

The size of the opportunity

Google is the only channel that will tell a client how big their in-market demand actually is. If the whole high-intent keyword set is 900 searches a month, no budget produces 50 demos from search. Uncomfortable, useful, and the strongest argument for demand gen there is.

Question 7 · continued

Three wrong turns on the way to the answer

Each of these is the obvious read. Each one is wrong, and each one costs real money.

Wrong turn 1

"June collapsed, so the ads stopped working." The leads were in the CRM the whole time — Google just wasn't recording them. Check tracking before you conclude anything about performance, or you will spend a month fixing a problem that doesn't exist.

Wrong turn 2

"This ad group got zero leads in 90 days — pause it." Not always. One ad group had a 7.3% CTR at around $10 a click and no leads. That is qualified traffic landing on the site and entering retargeting. We kept it deliberately.

Wrong turn 3

"Brand produces leads at $141 — move budget there." You cannot buy more brand demand on Google. Only as many people search the name as search it. Growing that number is what LinkedIn and content are for.

Question 8

One question to be able to answer before any client call

"Do I know why the number is what it is — and can I name the one thing we're changing to move it?"

Clients do not need a Google Ads specialist. They need someone who knows what happened and why, and who does not get surprised. Answer that question and you can hold any Google conversation.

Three checks before the call
  • Is the data clean? Steady week, right conversion actions. If not, that is the headline — not the cost per lead.
  • Which link broke? Query, click, page, form or tracking. Name one.
  • What are we changing, and what moves first? Usually cost per lead before volume. Say it before they ask.
The habits that build the trust
  • Never a number without a benchmark and a verdict. "6.37% — the benchmark is 3 to 5% — so we're above it." Value, range, meaning.
  • Raise the bad news first. Underspend, a paused campaign, a soft week — framed as a decision. It buys you the benefit of the doubt on everything else.
  • Ask about lead quality every call. "Did any of these move through the funnel?" It is the only real measure of whether we're doing a good job.
  • Don't overpromise. Commit to what you're confident in, then beat it.
  • Turn objections into segment questions. "$500 a lead is too expensive" is not a number to defend — it's a prompt to ask which segment is worth $500.

Part 2

How to walk a client through the month

The order matters more than the slides. It puts the channel's job before its numbers, the numbers before the verdict, and the verdict before the ask.

01
Frame it, and invite interruption

"The report is yours after the call — stop me anywhere." Then say what the channel is for: capturing demand from people already searching.

02
Five headline numbers, each with a range and a verdict

Spend, leads, cost per lead, CTR, conversion rate. Never a bare number. Resist the dashboard dump.

03
Raise the bad news yourself

"We spent $4,500 of the $5,000 on purpose — the last $500 would have pushed cost per lead up." Raising it first builds trust. Them finding it destroys it.

04
Split brand and non-brand

And answer the obvious question before it's asked: brand looks better because brand is limited. Far fewer people search the name, and non-brand is simply harder.

05
Where the money went, and what got paused

"This ad group spent $763 over 90 days with no leads, so we paused it and moved the budget to what's converting." Every pause needs a reason.

06
What we learned about their market

Which words converted, which phrasing buyers prefer, who else is bidding. This is the part they cannot get anywhere else.

07
Ask about lead quality

"How did these leads look? Did any move through the funnel?" It turns the client into a collaborator and gives you what you need to defend cost per lead next month.

08
What's changing next, and what moves first

Two or three actions with a date. After a structural change, cost per lead usually moves before volume — say that now, not in four weeks.

09
The budget ask — only with evidence

Needs all three: conversion volume, confirmed lead quality, and impression share headroom on something converting. "This theme converts at $180 and we're visible for 38% of those searches — here's what closing that gap costs."

The shape every finding takes

Observation → why it matters to their business → what we're doing and what to expect. No jargon survives that structure, and nobody leaves the call unsure what happens next.

Part 2 · The hard moments

Four things a client will say

Notice the pattern in all four answers: don't defend the number. Move to segment, timeline, or the boundary of what the channel controls.

"The phone isn't ringing."

Don't argue with the feeling. Acknowledge it, move to what the account has been doing and what changes this month, then ask what they are seeing come through. Nine times out of ten the gap between their inbox and the dashboard is attribution or form routing.

"$500 a lead isn't worth it for us."

If their unit economics say so, concede immediately — arguing with a client's own maths costs you credibility. Then turn it into a segment question: which segment is worth $500, and which is worth $150? That conversation produces a better account.

"These leads were bad."

Get specifics, and be honest about the limit: on search we buy the query, not the person. Then show the levers we do have — negatives, tighter match types, audience exclusions, a business-email gate on the form. Give a concrete change and a date. Promise a tighter filter, never perfect quality.

"Should we just put it all into Google?"

Answer with the ceiling. Non-brand is capped by how many people search the category; brand by how many have heard of them. Cutting demand gen shrinks the ceiling of the channel they're doubling down on. Show the search volume number — it's the most honest argument there is.

And one to say before they do

"Here's what I wasn't happy with this month." Naming your own miss costs very little and buys a lot of latitude on strategy. Clients forgive process problems. They do not forgive being managed.
